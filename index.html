What this is

When a subsea module is lowered onto a template or mudmat, vessel heave means it arrives with real velocity — typically 0.3 to 0.5 m/s, sometimes more. Hitting a steel foundation at that speed generates a load spike several times the module's submerged weight and can damage connectors, guideposts and soft soil.

The fix is a passive damper filled with the one fluid available in unlimited quantity at 300 m depth: seawater. The cylinder fills through a check valve as it extends on the way down. On contact, that check valve slams shut and the trapped water can only leave through a metered path — a small fixed orifice, backed by a relief valve that opens for the initial spike. Commercial units are marketed as soft-landing cylinders, and the patent literature describes exactly this arrangement.

The published behaviour is a two-phase stop: a short, hard shock-absorption phase where the relief valve caps the pressure, then a long, quiet soft-landing phase where the fixed orifice bleeds the remaining stroke at nearly constant force, taking the module from 0.5 m/s down to roughly 0.04 m/s over two or three seconds. Peak landing load drops by around 90 percent.

Both views are drawn to the same conventions. Cut metal is hatched, adjacent parts hatched the other way so the joints read; the rod is left solid because shafts are not sectioned. Detail A on the landing view is the leg the enlarged panel refers to, and the panel pairs the cylinder in section with the circuit it is plumbed into — the same pairing you get full size on the lock tab.

The model

Both modes run the same lumped-parameter chamber. Compression drives flow out of the cap volume; the valves set how easily it leaves; whatever cannot leave compresses the water itself.

dp/dt = (βeff / V) · ( Ap·u − Qline − Qrelief − Qcheck )

That one equation covers the whole story. With the metering path open, the pressure term equilibrates almost instantly and you recover the classic square-law damper, F ∝ u². With every path shut, the flow terms go to zero and the same equation becomes a very stiff spring of rate k = βA²/V. Nothing is switched; the physics changes character on its own.

The metering term is a state rather than a function of pressure, because the water in the line has mass — see Why closing time matters. The relief poppet and the refill check sit on the manifold block and stay quasi-steady:

Q = Cd·A(t)·√(2Δp/ρ)

Effective bulk modulus follows seawater at 2.34 GPa, corrected for entrained gas by 1/β = (1−x)/βw + x/pabs with the gas fraction shrinking isothermally as pressure rises. Integration is semi-implicit in pressure, which is what keeps the near-closed valve stable at a 50 µs step.

Stopping the flow

There are two ways to stop it in Hydraulic lock, and they are not the same thing. Shut valve drives the metering needle onto its seat: the compression path closes, but the refill check valve is still there, so the cylinder is held one way and free the other. Port block shuts a pair of pilot-operated check valves mounted on the cylinder port itself, upstream of everything, so the chamber is isolated from the whole manifold — relief included — in both directions. That is how load-holding lock valves are actually plumbed: a burst hose must not be able to drop the load.

Holding in extension only means something if the cylinder can pull, so the bench rod is bolted to the anvil. Turn Rod bolted off and you get the honest alternative: the chamber still cannot take water in, but the rig simply lifts off the anvil, and the lock holds nothing.

What a real block gives you is a spring made of water. The piston moves a fraction of a millimetre rather than the full 600 mm stroke, and the load bounces — a sealed chamber stores energy elastically and hands it straight back, where an orifice turns it into heat. The dissipated and returned readouts split that budget for you.

Nothing here seals perfectly. Seat leakage is a real valve specification, and at anything above zero a held load does not stop — it creeps, at a rate the readout reports in millimetres per minute. Set it to zero for the textbook answer; leave it at 0.02 % for something closer to a metal seat.

The lock is also a good way to see what "incompressible" really costs. At 300 bar the trapped water has given up about 1.3 percent of its volume — small, but not zero, and on a 17-litre chamber that is the whole of the piston travel you can see. Dial in even half a percent of entrained air and the assumption falls apart entirely: the strut goes soft at low pressure and only stiffens up once the bubbles have been squeezed out of existence. That soft-then-hard curve is a real commissioning problem, and it is why these cylinders are bled carefully.

Why closing time matters

A valve that shuts instantly is a modelling convenience that hides the most important consequence of shutting one. The metering path here carries the inertia of the water column in the line, so flow is a state of its own rather than an instantaneous function of pressure:

(ρL/Aline) · dQ/dt = p − Δpvalve(Q) − Δpline(Q)

Decelerating that column against a closing valve is what produces the pressure surge. It is not added on top of the model — it falls out of it, and the size of it depends on how the closing time compares with the line's own period, 2L/c.

Shut the default 1.5 m line in 2 ms and the surge reaches most of the Joukowsky bound ρcΔv, because the far end of the column has no idea yet that the valve has moved. Take 600 ms over it and there is no surge at all: the pressure wave has time to run back up the line and relieve. Lengthen the line and the same closure gets worse. This is the whole reason valve closing rates are specified in hydraulic circuits.

The stem itself does not move linearly either. It follows a modified equal-percentage characteristic with a rangeability of 50, so half travel is only about 12 percent of full flow area and almost all the useful metering happens in the last part of the stroke — which is why the commanded-opening slider feels so unevenly weighted, and why real needle valves are trimmed the way they are.

The valves themselves

The Valve detail tab opens all three up in section, and their internals move with the live state rather than being a static picture.

POC-104A/B is the load-holding lock. A ball check blocks one way only, so one cartridge alone would hold the load up but let the cylinder be pulled apart — which is why there are two, fitted back to back. Each is opened by pressure on the other side through a cross pilot; vent that pilot and both balls drop onto their seats at once. Watch the pushrods retract and the balls seat when you hit Port block.

NV-101 is the needle. The cone lifts off its seat with commanded travel, and the plot beside it is the trim characteristic — modified equal percentage, R = 50 — with the live operating point marked, which is where the "half travel is only 12 percent of area" behaviour comes from.

PSV-102 is the relief. The poppet lifts against its spring once inlet pressure passes the setting, and its plot shows flow against inlet pressure with the cracking point and the override slope. Engage the port block and it goes isolated, because it sits downstream of the lock.

The P&ID

The P&ID tab is the same circuit as an engineering sheet: tagged equipment and valves, line numbers with size and spec, instrument bubbles, a valve schedule, notes, legend and a title block.

It is live. PI-105 reads chamber pressure, ZT-107 reads piston travel, FI-108 reads line flow, the pipe runs animate in the direction the water is actually going, and the status column of the valve schedule updates as valves open, seat and isolate. Shut the port block and you can watch the whole sheet go dead downstream of POC-104A/B.

Tags are consistent across every view, so the needle you see in section as NV-101 is the same one that appears on the circuit diagram and in the schedule.

Sizing is deliberately close to the published devices: the 5.5 mm default orifice is what falls out of asking for a 0.05 m/s terminal creep under the module's submerged weight, and it lands squarely in the 3–10 mm range quoted in the patent.

What is simplified

One vertical degree of freedom — no pitch, roll, pendulum swing or off-centre landing, all of which matter in a real analysis. Added mass is a constant multiple rather than the depth- and proximity-dependent coefficient DNV-RP-N103 would have you use, and the sharp rise in added mass as a mudmat approaches the seabed is not modelled. Soil is a rigid foundation, so there is no bearing failure, no suction on retrieval and no water entrapment under the mudmat. Seal friction is a single Coulomb term.

The line is a single lumped inertance, not a distributed wave model, so it gives the right surge magnitude and the right dependence on closing time but not the reflected wave train that follows. Relief and check valves are quasi-steady, so poppet chatter does not appear. Cavitation is a pressure clamp at the vapour point, not a two-phase model: the readout will tell you the water has boiled, but the subsequent void collapse — which is the part that damages hardware — is not simulated.

It is a teaching model with defensible numbers, not an installation analysis.
