What this is

When a subsea module is lowered onto a template or mudmat, vessel heave means it arrives with real velocity — typically 0.3 to 0.5 m/s, sometimes more. Hitting a steel foundation at that speed generates a load spike several times the module's submerged weight and can damage connectors, guideposts and soft soil.

The fix is a passive damper filled with the one fluid available in unlimited quantity at 300 m depth: seawater. The cylinder fills through a check valve as it extends on the way down. On contact, that check valve slams shut and the trapped water can only leave through a metered path — a small fixed orifice, backed by a relief valve that opens for the initial spike. Commercial units are marketed as soft-landing cylinders, and the patent literature describes exactly this arrangement.

The published behaviour is a two-phase stop: a short, hard shock-absorption phase where the relief valve caps the pressure, then a long, quiet soft-landing phase where the fixed orifice bleeds the remaining stroke at nearly constant force, taking the module from 0.5 m/s down to roughly 0.04 m/s over two or three seconds. Peak landing load drops by around 90 percent.

The model

Both modes run the same lumped-parameter chamber. Compression drives flow out of the cap volume; the orifice and relief valve set how easily it leaves; whatever cannot leave compresses the water itself.

dp/dt = (βeff / V) · ( Ap·u − Qout(p) )
Qout = Cd·Ao·√(2p/ρ)  +  Cd·Ar·√(2(p−pset)/ρ)

That single equation covers the whole story. With the orifice open, the pressure term equilibrates almost instantly and you recover the classic square-law damper, F ∝ u². With the orifice shut, Qout = 0 and the same equation becomes a very stiff spring of rate k = βA²/V. Nothing is switched; the physics changes character on its own.

Effective bulk modulus follows seawater at 2.34 GPa, corrected for entrained gas by 1/β = (1−x)/βw + x/pabs with the gas fraction shrinking isothermally as pressure rises. Integration is semi-implicit in pressure, which is what keeps the near-closed valve stable at a 50 µs step.

Stopping the flow

Switch to Hydraulic lock and close the metering valve, or hit Block ports to shut the pilot-operated check valve as well. The water now has nowhere to go, and the damper stops being a damper.

What you get instead is a spring made of water. At the default bench setting the piston moves a few millimetres rather than the full 600 mm stroke, chamber pressure jumps to several hundred bar, and the load bounces — because a locked chamber stores energy elastically and gives it straight back, where an orifice turns it into heat. The dissipated and returned readouts split that budget for you.

The lock is also a good way to see what "incompressible" really costs. At 300 bar the trapped water has given up about 1.3 percent of its volume — small, but not zero, and on a 17-litre chamber that is the whole of the piston travel you can see. Dial in even half a percent of entrained air and the assumption falls apart entirely: the strut goes soft at low pressure and only stiffens up once the bubbles have been squeezed out of existence. That soft-then-hard curve is a real commissioning problem, and it is why these cylinders are bled carefully.

Sizing is deliberately close to the published devices: the 5.5 mm default orifice is what falls out of asking for a 0.05 m/s terminal creep under the module's submerged weight, and it lands squarely in the 3–10 mm range quoted in the patent.

What is simplified

One vertical degree of freedom — no pitch, roll, pendulum swing or off-centre landing, all of which matter in a real analysis. Added mass is a constant multiple rather than the depth- and proximity-dependent coefficient DNV-RP-N103 would have you use, and the sharp rise in added mass as a mudmat approaches the seabed is not modelled. Soil is a rigid foundation, so there is no bearing failure, no suction on retrieval and no water entrapment under the mudmat. Valve dynamics are quasi-steady, so relief-valve chatter and genuine distributed water hammer do not appear; the Joukowsky figure shown on the lock readout is an indicator, not a solved transient. Seal friction is a single Coulomb term.

It is a teaching model with defensible numbers, not an installation analysis.
